## Introduction: When Film Becomes a "Financial Asset"

If you've been following the film market from late 2025 into early 2026, one thing is unmistakable: film prices have risen again. And this time, the scale and scope of the increases are substantial. For many film photography enthusiasts, this isn't just a simple cost increase—it directly impacts our ability to keep shooting happily and consistently. Kodak Alaris announced price hikes for most of its film stocks starting February 2026, with increases ranging from a modest 1% to a staggering 39% [1]. Meanwhile, brands like Fuji and Ilford have also adjusted their prices. Faced with this ongoing wave of price hikes, should we panic-buy or wait rationally? This article aims to break down the causes and effects of this price surge from a practical standpoint and provide a set of actionable strategies to save money.

![Price Hikes: Who Got Hit?](https://media.openfilm.cc/images/post-20260817-28/infographic-02-en.webp)
## 1. The 2026 Price Hike Landscape: Which Films Saw the Biggest Increases?

To formulate a strategy, you first need to understand the situation. According to data compiled by Analogspace.nl, Kodak Alaris's price adjustments cover a wide product range from entry-level to professional. Here are some key products and their price increases, giving you a direct sense of the impact:

*   **"Hardest Hit" Products**: Films with increases over 20% are at the forefront. For example, Kodak Pro Image 100 (38.7%), Kodak Gold 200 120 (21.2%), T-Max 100 120 (20%), and Ektachrome E100 120 (19.4%). These films are either value-for-money choices or essential in specific formats (like 120 rolls), and the price hikes directly affect shooting budgets [1].
*   **Professional and Black & White Films**: Professional color negative films like Portra 160/400 120 and Ektar 100 120 saw increases between 3% and 14.4%. Classic black and white films like Tri-X 400 120 (1.2%) and T-Max 3200 (2.7%) had relatively moderate increases, but still represent added costs [1].
*   **135 Format Relatively Stable**: Notably, some 135 format films saw smaller increases, such as Portra 160 135 (2.6%) and Ektar 100 135 (1%). This may be related to market competition and consumer habits.

**Root Cause of the Hikes**: The official reason given is "significant increases in the price of silver and other raw materials." This is not an excuse. According to the World Silver Survey 2025, silver prices surged from around $30 per ounce to over $80 per ounce in 2025, an increase of nearly 140% [2]. The photography industry consumes approximately 25.5 million ounces of silver annually. As a core component of silver halide emulsions, the skyrocketing price of silver directly drove up the production cost of film. This is a global raw material cost crisis, not a market action by a single brand.

## 2. Eastman Kodak Direct Supply vs. Kodak Alaris Distribution: A Shift in Pricing Structure

A key variable in this price surge is **Eastman Kodak's direct supply system**. Simply put, the original "Kodak company" (Eastman Kodak) has begun bypassing its independent distributor, Kodak Alaris, to sell some film stocks directly [2][4].

**What does this mean?**

*   **Price Differences**: Direct-supply versions are typically priced slightly lower than Alaris versions. For example, Eastman Kodak's direct-supply Gold 200 five-pack sells for $39.95, while the Alaris version is $42.95; Ektachrome E100 direct-supply is about $22, versus $24 for the Alaris version [2]. In the context of widespread price increases, this small difference is particularly valuable.
*   **Product Range**: Currently, products distributed directly by Eastman Kodak include Kodacolor 100/200, Gold 200, Ultramax 400, Tri-X 400, Ektar 100, and Ektachrome E100 [2]. This means these films now have two parallel supply channels and pricing systems.
*   **Geographic Limitations**: Currently, Eastman Kodak's direct supply service is primarily limited to the US and Canadian markets. In other regions like Europe, film is still mainly distributed through Kodak Alaris [2].

**Impact on Purchasing Strategy**: If you're in North America, buying direct-supply Kodak film is the more economical choice. This also means that Kodak Portra series professional negative films are currently the only professional Kodak films still primarily distributed by Kodak Alaris [4]. Understanding this channel change is the first step to saving money.

## 3. Stockpiling Strategy: What to Buy, Where to Store, and How Long to Keep?

Facing price hikes, stockpiling is the most direct response. But stockpiling isn't about blind hoarding—it's a skill that requires technique.

### Films Worth Prioritizing for Stockpiling

1.  **Films with Large Price Increases and High Usage**: Like Gold 200 120 (+21.2%) and Pro Image 100 (+38.7%). If you use these regularly, buying them now at the old price is a win.
2.  **Films with Significant Direct-Supply Price Advantages**: Like Ektachrome E100 and Ektar 100. Watch for new releases and promotions from the Eastman Kodak direct-supply channel.
3.  **Long-Shelf-Life and Stable Black & White Films**: Like Tri-X 400 and HP5+. Black and white negative films are less sensitive to expiration, and Ilford's price increases are relatively moderate, making them stable "hard currency."
4.  **Unique Films with No Direct Substitutes**: For example, Ektachrome E100, as the only currently produced daylight-balanced color reversal film, its value is self-evident. Stock up some to guard against future price hikes or supply shortages.

### Films to Consider Waiting On or Finding Alternatives For

1.  **Films with Small Price Increases or Already Having Direct-Supply Channels**: Like some 135 format films.
2.  **Films with Clear Alternatives**: For example, for everyday documentation, you can use cheaper cinema films (requiring ECN-2 processing) or domestic films to replace some color negative functions.
3.  **Professional Films Already at High Prices**: Like the Portra series, unless necessary, consider using other negative films with different color characteristics as substitutes.

### Storage and Shelf Life for Stockpiled Film

*   **Storage Environment is Key**: Film's greatest enemies are heat and humidity. Unopened film should be stored in the **refrigerator compartment (around 4°C)** or even the freezer. Refrigeration significantly slows emulsion aging, preserving color and sensitivity.
*   **Acclimatize Before Use**: Film taken from the refrigerator must be left at room temperature for several hours to fully return to room temperature and avoid surface condensation before use.
*   **Expiration Dates Aren't Absolute**: The "expiration date" on film packaging typically refers to the period during which optimal performance can be guaranteed under recommended storage conditions. Properly refrigerated film often shows little performance change even a year or two past expiration. However, the longer past expiration, the greater the uncertainty. It's recommended to test-shoot a roll first.

## 4. Alternative Approaches: Broadening Your Arsenal

Beyond stockpiling original film, smart alternatives can effectively control costs.

### Cinema Films (ECN-2 Process)

Cinema films (like the Kodak Vision3 series) are a money-saving secret for many experienced shooters. They share emulsion technology with consumer films but are often priced lower. Note the following:
*   **Different Processing**: Cinema films require professional ECN-2 processing, which uses different chemicals and processes than consumer C-41. Many labs in China now offer this service.
*   **Base and Remjet Layer**: Cinema films have a carbon-black Remjet backing layer that requires special treatment before processing. When purchasing, choose versions with the Remjet already removed, or confirm the lab can handle it.
*   **Color Characteristics**: Cinema film color grading is tailored more towards cinematic needs and may require post-processing to achieve a "traditional" photo look, but this itself can be a unique creative style.

### Domestic Films: Lucky Film, etc.

China's Lucky Film was once a proud national brand, and in recent years, some enthusiasts have revisited its products. While its product line and performance may lag behind Kodak and Fuji, in specific scenarios (like practice or experimental shooting), its extremely low price offers a stress-free shooting experience. Additionally, some new domestic repackaging brands have emerged on the market, which can be options for trying something new and low-cost practice.

### Harman Phoenix 200 II: A New Contender

Harman's (Ilford's parent company) Phoenix 200 II color negative film offers a non-Japanese alternative to the market. Priced at around $11.99 (35mm 36 exposures), it has a clear value-for-money advantage in the 2026 market [9]. It features a unique grain structure and a slightly magenta-toned cool color palette, suitable for creators seeking stylized expression. Incorporating it as part of your regular film diet can effectively reduce reliance on traditional brands and alleviate cost pressure.

## 5. Long-Term Perspective: The Philosophy of Cost Control in Film Photography

Price hikes are a reality, but the joy of film photography shouldn't be entirely stifled by cost. Developing a long-term philosophy of cost control is more important than simply stockpiling.

*   **Shoot Deliberately, Not Blindly**: Expensive film actually encourages us to treat each shutter press more seriously. Learning metering, composition, and thinking about the frame to improve your "hit rate" is itself a refinement of photographic skill.
*   **Build a Diversified "Arsenal"**: Don't rely on just one or two film stocks. Based on subject, lighting, and budget, flexibly combine Kodak, Fuji, Ilford, Harman, cinema films, and domestic films. The different characteristics of various films can also inspire different creative ideas.
*   **Explore Second-Hand and Swaps**: In the film photography community, second-hand trading and swapping of film rolls are common. Sometimes you can find well-preserved older batches at good prices.
*   **Enjoy the Process, Not Anxiety Over Results**: One of the charms of film photography lies in its uncertainty and the waiting process. Shifting your focus from "how much did this roll cost me" to "what unique moments can I capture with this roll" will make the experience much more relaxed.

## Conclusion

The 2026 film price surge is the result of combined factors: raw material costs and changes in market structure. As enthusiasts, we cannot change the broader environment, but we can proactively manage our shooting costs by **understanding the details of price increases, utilizing direct-supply channels, implementing scientific stockpiling, and exploring alternatives**. Remember, the core of film photography is creation and documentation, not anxiety over consumable prices. Use smart strategies to control costs, then devote more energy to discovering and capturing those moments worth freezing in time.
